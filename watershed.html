Bengaluru water price · Who should Bengaluru pay? · October 2026

Should Bengaluru pay the places its water comes from?

Yes: about ₹1.4 per kilolitre, or ₹23 a month for a family. But the city owes more downstream, and that comes first.

The usual argument is that if Bengaluru pays the people of Kodagu to look after their forests, the city will get more water. It won't. Bengaluru's summer water is monsoon water stored in the KRS and Kabini dams. In a drought, Karnataka keeps the city's taps running by cutting water to farmers in Mandya and to Tamil Nadu. Healthier forests upstream would mostly send more water to those farmers and to Tamil Nadu.

There are better reasons to pay. The city takes its water from hills it pays nothing to keep, and new dams and pipelines are being built there in its name. A fair share of the cost of keeping those hills working is about ₹70 crore a year. But the city's own sewage is poisoning Byramangala and the Thenpennai river into Tamil Nadu, and its old river, the Arkavathy, has dried up. Those debts are larger and come first.

About 60% of the city's water is Cauvery water. BWSSB takes it at the Shiva Anicut, just above the Shivanasamudram falls, and pumps it about 100 km and 300–500 m uphill in three stages. That water drains from about 21,000 km² of hills: Kodagu (through the Harangi dam), Hassan and Chikkamagaluru (the Hemavathi), the plains above the KRS dam, and Wayanad in Kerala (the Kabini). The other 40% comes from borewells under the city, pumped about nine times faster than rain refills them.

Units used on this page: TMC is a thousand million cubic feet, about 28 billion litres, or roughly two weeks of the city's Cauvery supply. MLD is a million litres a day. A kL is a kilolitre, 1,000 litres.

Which dam, which month? Nobody publishes this. In April 2026 the state was releasing about 300 cusecs a day from KRS for Bengaluru, roughly 9.5 TMC a year. The rest of the city's 29 TMC comes from Kabini when KRS water goes to irrigation. So Wayanad, in Kerala, may supply half the city's water in a normal year. Cauvery Stage VI will move part of the intake up to KRS. That water will come only from Kodagu, Hassan and Chikkamagaluru, and it will be taken above Mysuru's sewage.

The legal share. The Supreme Court's 2018 judgment gives Karnataka 284.75 TMC, including 4.75 TMC more for Bengaluru's drinking water. The state lets BWSSB draw about 29 TMC a year, rising to 35 TMC with Stage VI. Any honest "pay the source" scheme has to include Wayanad, which means two state governments, not just BWSSB.

From January to May, Bengaluru draws about 10 TMC. In those same months the rivers flowing into KRS and Kabini bring only about 2–7 TMC. So almost all of the city's summer water is monsoon water held in the dams since the previous year. What matters for Bengaluru is how much rain the monsoon brings, how much the dams can still hold after silting, and who gets priority when there isn't enough. The trickle from Kodagu's streams in March matters much less.

On 1 January, KRS and Kabini together usually hold 20–45 TMC of usable water. Bengaluru's 10 TMC for the summer comes out of that store, alongside 12.76 TMC that Karnataka must send to Tamil Nadu between January and May, and the drinking water of Mysuru, Mandya and other towns.

Forests could matter to this store in two ways: by helping the late monsoon soak in and drain slowly into the dams, and by keeping soil, and silt, on the hills. Studies of small catchments in the Western Ghats show both. But at the scale of KRS, no study has yet found a change in inflow or silt that can be traced to Kodagu's forest loss. What has changed is how much is drawn upstream: canals and farm ponds. So a payment scheme should aim at keeping intact forest, grassland, paddy, wetlands and stable slopes, not at planting more trees.

Kodagu's evergreen forest fell from 40.5% of the district in 1973 to 24.2% in 2018. Most of that went between 1991 and 2010, when coffee boomed. Paddy fields, which act as wetlands, fell from 35,000 ha to between 14,000 and 23,000 ha. Wayanad's fell from 30,000–40,000 ha to between 8,000 and 19,000, depending on who counts. Landslides killed 20–26 people in Kodagu in 2018 and about 330 in Wayanad in 2024. Total rainfall in Kodagu has not fallen, but the rainy season is ending earlier.

Most of the damage in Kodagu and Wayanad isn't caused by Bengaluru's water draw. Coffee markets, irrigation, roads, homestays and weak state rules drove it. Irrigation alone takes more than 80% of the water drawn above KRS. The harm Bengaluru does cause is at its intake and below it. In the dry months it takes about as much water as Karnataka owes Tamil Nadu, just above the Shivanasamudram gorge. Its sewage poisons Byramangala and the Thenpennai. And new dams are justified in its name.

The ledger points one way. Bengaluru's clearest debts are to the people downstream of its sewage and to its own old river, the Arkavathy. Its debt to Kodagu and Wayanad is real but smaller and less direct.

Its sewage, its leaks and its own old river, the Arkavathy. Closing the city's sewage gap costs about ₹1,900 crore to build, or about ₹2.3 per kL billed once the plants are paid off over their life. That would stop the harm it is doing to Byramangala and Tamil Nadu. ₹100 crore spent fixing leaking pipes saves about 27 million litres a day. The same money buys 7 MLD from Stage VI and 2–3 from Mekedatu. Paying Kodagu would yield no amount you could measure. Paying farmers around TG Halli to give up eucalyptus and borewells is the one watershed payment likely to bring water back.

The home options are harder than they look. TG Halli holds 2.2 of its 3.3 TMC today, but the city doesn't use it, because people don't trust the water. Fixing leaks also cuts the water that leaks recharge into the aquifer. And keeping more treated water in the city would cut what Kolar's farmers now receive. None of these is a reason to look upstream first.

The order we'd suggest: close the sewage gap; fund the home watershed, including the Arkavathy farmers; then make a modest upstream payment, and say plainly that it is paid because it is fair and as insurance, not to buy water.

Almost none, for three reasons. First, the city's taps are already protected: in the 2016-17 and 2023-24 droughts Karnataka kept Bengaluru's Cauvery water flowing by cutting farmers and Tamil Nadu. Second, extra water from a healthier catchment would mostly go to Tamil Nadu and Karnataka's farms. Third, planting trees often reduces river flow. Protecting natural forest, grassland, paddy and wetland helps; planting trees on grassland or farmland usually hurts.

The droughts. In 2023-24 Tamil Nadu received 81 of the 177 TMC it was owed, and farmers in Mandya, Mysuru and Kodagu got no water for summer crops. Bengaluru kept drawing about 1,450 MLD from the Cauvery (it draws about 1,900 now, after Stage V). Its shortage came from its own borewells: 6,900 of 14,000 public borewells ran dry. Nothing in law guarantees this protection. The court put drinking water "on a higher pedestal", but no written rule exempts the city when the shared water is cut; it is a political choice made each time.

Who gets extra water. In normal years almost all of it flows on to Tamil Nadu, turning the Shivanasamudram turbines on the way. Bengaluru gains nothing, because its pumps cap what it can draw, not the river. In a drought year, our rough split is about 55% to Tamil Nadu, 30% to Karnataka's farms, 8–10% to Bengaluru and 4–5% to other towns. Extra water in June and July also helps Karnataka meet what it owes Tamil Nadu in those months, which is a reason for the state, more than the city, to pay.

Trees and water. Studies in the Western Ghats find that natural forest keeps streams flowing longer into the dry season than degraded forest or acacia plantations. But planting trees on grassland cuts runoff by about 44%, and by 75% with eucalyptus, and in about 80% of reforestation studies water yield fell. Modelling of the upper Cauvery adds a warning: more farm ponds and check dams upstream would cut inflow to KRS by 2–6%, and by 10–15% in dry years.

Silt and quality. KRS has lost about 7% of its capacity since 1932 and Kabini about 5% since 1974, which is slow by Indian standards. Catchment work might save ₹3–10 crore a year of lost storage, for all users together. The exception may be Harangi, Kodagu's own dam: its engineers say the 2018 landslides dumped about 1.2 TMC of silt into it, but nobody has surveyed it. The water at the city's intake is clean on the measures available: treatment chemicals cost about ₹0.40 per kL, a small fraction of the cost of pumping it uphill.

Three reasons hold up. It's fair: the city lives off hills it pays nothing for, and new dams and pipelines are built there in its name. It's insurance: in a bad year like 2016-17, when KRS fell below its minimum level, the city depended on what the monsoon left in the dams, and the evidence from small catchments says forests, soils and slopes help keep that store up. It keeps the land: paying keeps alive the paddy, groves and native shade that would otherwise turn into arecanut, silver oak and resorts. None of these is buying water, so the payment should be sized as a fair contribution, not as a price for litres.

There are two different reasons to hand over money, and they call for different sums. One is paying for something you get, like less silt in the dams. The other is paying because it is fair, since the city takes and gives nothing back. One number cannot do both jobs.

Measured as a service, the upstream payment is worth very little to the city. Less silt might save all users ₹3–10 crore a year; Bengaluru's share of that is about ₹1 crore. So of the ₹70 crore Bengaluru would pay under this page's scheme, about ₹44 crore goes to harm the city causes or water it can get back (sewage plants above its intake, Arkavathy farmers), and about ₹26 crore is a fair share of keeping the hills working.

One argument does not hold up: "pay Kodagu so Bengaluru gets more water". If a scheme is sold that way, people will judge it on litres. It will fail that test, and it will lose public support.

"The community" is really four groups, and a payment per hectare reaches only one of them. Landholders, mostly smallholders, can be paid to keep native shade and stream banks. Paddy owners can be paid to keep their fields as wetlands, as Kerala already does. Village committees can be funded to protect sacred groves and community forests. Landless adivasi and plantation workers, who own nothing to be paid for, can be paid to do the work.

A law change would do more than money. Silver oak spreads because planters may fell it freely while the state owns the native trees on most farms. Giving landholders rights over native trees they plant or protect, while still protecting large old trees, would fix much of the shade problem for almost nothing.

What money can't do. Stopping a sale to a resort would take ₹5–10 lakh per hectare a year. That is unaffordable, so rules have to do that job, and the legislature's vote against the eco-sensitive area draft makes that harder.

Ask them first. This page has not asked the people of Kodagu and Wayanad what they want. The loudest local demands in the news are about tree rights, elephants and the eco-sensitive area rules. A scheme should be designed with them, not for them.

How it compares. New York spends about $100 million a year on its watershed, about ₹7 per kL. Quito puts 2% of water bills into its water fund; Lima about 0.5%. Bengaluru's share here is about 3% of its revenue, a little above Quito. It is about 1% of what the main page finds a kL really costs to supply (₹113).

Not Bengaluru alone. Mandya's farmers and Tamil Nadu gain more from healthy hills than the city does. A flat charge per kL would weigh most on the poorest homes: ₹1.4 is 16% on top of the first slab's ₹8.50 per kL, and about 1% for bulk users at ₹99. A charge set as a share of each bill raises the same money and is fairer. Companies can also give through the same trust as part of their water-stewardship commitments.

What about Tamil Nadu and Mandya? They take most of any extra water from the hills, so in fairness they should pay too. No body exists that could charge them. The Cauvery Water Management Authority sets releases, not payments. A basin-wide scheme is the right end point; a Karnataka scheme with Bengaluru paying its share is the place to start.

Through an independent trust, paying only when forest, paddy and groves are still there. Section 16(1)(d) of the BWSSB Act lets the board's charges pay for things that improve water supply in the city. That covers the sewage plants above the intake and the Arkavathy farmers, about ₹44 crore. The fair share for the hills, about ₹26 crore, needs either the state budget or a one-line change to the Act that adds "protecting the sources of supply". Section 16(2) bars the board from building up a surplus, so the money must pass each year to a trust.

  • Spend the money; don't just collect it. Lima's utility has spent only about 7% of the money it set aside for its catchment, and across Peru about 75% of such money was unspent in 2025. Peru passed a decree in January 2026 forcing utilities to use it or hand it over. Quito's fund works better because an independent trust holds and spends it.
  • A contract between governments. China's Xin'an deal began in 2012 with ¥100 million a year from each province plus ¥300 million from Beijing, rose to ¥200 million, and since 2023 pools about ¥1 billion a year. Payment depends on water quality at the border, which has met the target every year since. Karnataka could make the same deal with Kodagu and Hassan, using silt and water quality at the Harangi and KRS inflows.
  • Pay people, not offices. In Vietnam's national scheme, households received only about 6% of payouts directly and forest boards about half, though communities also hold most of the protection contracts. Pay households and village committees directly. Use the Biodiversity Management Committees that already exist in every local body, and the gram sabhas. Karnataka has rejected 89% of forest-rights claims, so the gram sabha route is narrow for now.
  • Check on the ground, not just from space. Satellites can show paddy and forest cover, but not tell native shade from silver oak. So shade payments need a sample of farms checked on the ground each year. That costs more: budget up to 12–15% for that line.
  • Don't pay for trees counted. India's Green Credit scheme still rewards trees counted on Forest Department land and has no way to measure water. Paying for trees planted would reward the very planting that can cut dry-season flow.
  • Budget for checking. Monitoring and administration typically cost 7–11% of a scheme. A pair of stream gauges costs about $4,700. The first thing to buy is a silt survey of Harangi.
  • Kerala needs its own deal. BWSSB can build works anywhere in Karnataka, but not in Wayanad. The Kabini side needs an agreement between the two states, built on Kerala's existing paddy payments.
  • Plan for year eleven. When payments stop, people go back: 88% of Miyun's farmers said they would return to paddy. If the trust saves part of each year's money, as Quito's fund does, it can build an endowment that keeps paying after the first ten years.

Measured honestly, the scheme would buy land kept in working order. Over ten years that could mean:

  • native shade kept on 20,000 ha of coffee;
  • 28,000 ha of paddy and wetland still farmed;
  • the 1,214 sacred groves protected;
  • about 1,500 km of stream bank restored;
  • steady work for about 2,000 landless people;
  • eucalyptus and borewells retired on 5,000 ha of the Arkavathy catchment.

It would also mean somewhat less silt in the dams, cleaner water in the towns upstream, and some water back in the Arkavathy. It would not mean a number of extra litres at the city's Cauvery tap.

The biggest unknown is how much water each part of the basin gives in each month, and how changes in land use have changed it. No hydrographic survey of these reservoirs has been published since Kabini's in 2010; a 2019 satellite estimate puts Kabini's loss at 5% over 44 years, and nothing recent exists for KRS, Harangi or Hemavathi. The drought-year split of benefits, the Arkavathy payment rate and the cost of sewage plants upstream are our own estimates, and each is marked as such.

Data that would settle the biggest questions:

  • BWSSB's monthly draw, and which dam each month's water was released from.
  • Daily inflows to Harangi, KRS and Kabini over 2015–2026.
  • Reservoir silt surveys, starting with Harangi.
  • How much sewage Mysuru actually produces and treats.
  • Coffee Board cultivation costs for 2024-26.
  • Outcomes of forest-rights claims in Kodagu.
  • What the people of Kodagu and Wayanad want from a scheme.
  • A legal opinion on sections 16(1)(d), 16(2) and 127A of the BWSSB Act.

This page was built from eight pieces of research: where the water comes from, what harm is done, what a scheme would cost, how the money could move, and four follow-ups on drought rules, land ownership, the city's own watershed and the law. A separate AI model (Claude Fable) re-checked the first four against the original documents. Three more checks then read the finished page: one on hydrology and facts, one on the economics of paying for land, and one on plain language and design. Those checks changed:

  • Kodagu's forest loss: 40.5% to 24.2%, not 27.1%.
  • Wayanad's death toll: 330, not "over 400".
  • Mekedatu's water for Bengaluru: 5–7 TMC, not 67.
  • The aquifer: pumped about nine times faster than rain refills it, not five.
  • The scheme: Bengaluru now pays a share of the Wayanad line, the Arkavathy rate went up fivefold to a level that could change what farmers do, and stewards' pay rose to ₹600 a day. The total rose from ₹103 crore to about ₹123 crore.
  • The law: the BWSSB Act can carry the sewage and Arkavathy lines, but probably not the fair share for the hills.

They also changed the argument. The city's summer water is stored monsoon water, and the case for paying rests on fairness and insurance, not on extra litres.
