Bengaluru water price · Who should Bengaluru pay? · October 2026

Should Bengaluru pay the places its water comes from?

Yes, but not for the reason people usually give.

The usual argument is that if Bengaluru pays the people of Kodagu to look after their forests, the city will get more water. It won't. Bengaluru's summer water is monsoon water stored in the KRS and Kabini dams. In a drought, Karnataka keeps the city's taps running by cutting water to farmers in Mandya and to Tamil Nadu. Healthier forests upstream would mostly send more water to those farmers and to Tamil Nadu, not to Bengaluru.

The real reasons to pay are different. The city takes its water from a landscape it pays nothing to keep, and it asks that landscape to carry new dams and pipelines in its name. A fair payment for that is small: about ₹1 per kilolitre, or roughly ₹17 a month for a typical family. But the city owes more downstream than upstream. Its own sewage is poisoning Byramangala and the Thenpennai river into Tamil Nadu, and fixing that comes first.

About 60% of the city's water is Cauvery water. BWSSB takes it at the Shiva Anicut, just above the Shivanasamudram falls, and pumps it 100 km and 350 m uphill. That water drains from about 21,000 km² of hills: Kodagu (through the Harangi dam), Hassan and Chikkamagaluru (the Hemavathi), the plains above the KRS dam, and Wayanad in Kerala (the Kabini). The other 40% comes from borewells under the city itself, pumped about five times faster than rain refills them.

The legal pipe. The Supreme Court's 2018 Cauvery judgment gives Karnataka 284.75 TMC, including 4.75 TMC more for Bengaluru's drinking water. The state lets BWSSB draw about 29 TMC a year, rising to 35 TMC once Cauvery Stage VI is built. Stage VI moves part of the intake upstream to KRS. Yettinahole is meant to add 2.5 TMC from the Western Ghats, but the state auditor and IISc both say it will deliver far less than promised.

The inter-state part. Nearly all of the Kabini's water rises in Wayanad, in Kerala. Any honest "pay the source" scheme has to include Kerala's villages, which means two state governments, not just BWSSB.

From January to May, Bengaluru draws about 10 TMC. In those same months the rivers flowing into KRS and Kabini bring only about 2–7 TMC. So almost all of the city's summer water is monsoon water held in the dams since the previous year. What matters for Bengaluru is how much the monsoon delivers, how much of it the dams can still hold after silting, and who gets priority when there isn't enough. The trickle from Kodagu's streams in March matters much less.

On 1 January, KRS and Kabini together usually hold 20–45 TMC of usable water. Bengaluru's 10 TMC for the summer comes out of that store, alongside 12.76 TMC that Karnataka must send to Tamil Nadu between January and May, and the drinking water of Mysuru, Mandya and other towns. The forests upstream matter to this store in two ways. They help the late monsoon soak into the ground and drain slowly into the dams from August to November. And they keep soil, and the silt that would fill the dams, on the hills.

This changes the question. A payment scheme should aim at what keeps the monsoon soaking in and the silt out. That means intact forest, grassland, paddy fields and wetlands, and stable slopes. More trees are not the aim.

Kodagu's evergreen forest fell from 40.5% of the district in 1973 to 24.2% in 2018. Most of that went between 1991 and 2010, when coffee boomed. Paddy fields, which act as wetlands, fell from 35,000 ha to somewhere between 14,000 and 23,000 ha. Wayanad's fell from 30,000 ha to about 7,500. Landslides killed 20–26 people in Kodagu in 2018 and about 330 in Wayanad in 2024. Total rainfall in Kodagu has not fallen, but the rainy season is ending earlier.

Most of the damage in Kodagu and Wayanad isn't caused by Bengaluru's water draw. Coffee markets, irrigation, roads, homestays and weak state rules drove it. Irrigation alone takes more than 80% of the water drawn above KRS. The harms Bengaluru does cause sit at its intake and downstream of it. In the dry months it takes about as much water as Karnataka owes Tamil Nadu, just above the Shivanasamudram gorge. Its sewage poisons Byramangala and the Thenpennai. And new dams are justified in its name.

The ledger points one way. Bengaluru's clearest debts are to the people downstream of its sewage and to its own old watershed, the Arkavathy. Its debt to Kodagu is real but smaller and less direct.

Almost none, for three reasons. First, the city's taps are already protected: in the 2016-17 and 2023-24 droughts Karnataka kept Bengaluru's Cauvery water flowing by cutting farmers and Tamil Nadu. Second, extra water from a healthier catchment would mostly go to Tamil Nadu and Karnataka's farms. Third, planting trees often reduces river flow. Protecting natural forest, grassland, paddy and wetland helps; planting trees on grassland or farmland usually hurts.

The droughts. In 2023-24 Tamil Nadu received 81 of the 177 TMC it was owed, and farmers in Mandya, Mysuru and Kodagu got no water for summer crops. Bengaluru kept drawing about 1,450 MLD from the Cauvery. Its shortage came from its own borewells, as 6,900 of 14,000 public borewells ran dry. This protection is political, not legal. The court put drinking water "on a higher pedestal", but no written rule exempts the city when the shared water is cut.

Who gets extra water. In normal years almost all of it flows on to Tamil Nadu, turning the Shivanasamudram turbines on the way. Bengaluru gains nothing, because its own pumps cap its draw, not the river. In a drought year, our rough split is about 55% to Tamil Nadu, 30% to Karnataka's farms, 8–10% to Bengaluru and 4–5% to other towns.

Trees and water. Field studies in the Western Ghats find that natural forest keeps streams flowing longer into the dry season than degraded forest or acacia plantations. But planting trees on grassland cuts runoff by about 44%, and by 75% with eucalyptus. In about 80% of reforestation studies water yield fell. The newest reviews find dry-season gains only where degraded land is restored to natural forest, not where trees are planted on grassland or farmland. Modelling of the upper Cauvery adds a warning: more farm ponds and check dams upstream would cut inflow to KRS by 2–6%, and by 10–15% in dry years.

Silt and quality. Kabini has lost about 4% of its capacity in 36 years and KRS about 7% since 1932. That is slow by Indian standards. The storage lost each year is worth about ₹28–32 crore, and catchment work might save ₹3–10 crore of it. The water at the city's intake is clean: treatment chemicals cost about ₹0.40 per kL, against about ₹16 for power.

Three reasons hold up. Fairness: the city lives off a landscape it pays nothing for, and asks that landscape to carry new dams and pipelines in its name. Insurance: in a bad year like 2016-17, when KRS fell below its minimum level, the city depends on what the monsoon left in the dams, and that depends on forests, soils and slopes upstream. Stewardship: paying keeps alive the paddy, groves and native shade that would otherwise turn into arecanut, silver oak and resorts. None of these is "buying water", so the payment should be sized as a fair contribution, not as a price for litres.

Two rationales need to be kept apart. A service price pays for something the city gets, such as less silt in the dams or a steadier monsoon recharge. A justice transfer recognises that the city takes and gives nothing back. One rupee figure can't be both. This page sizes the scheme from what it would cost to keep the landscape working and then asks what share is fairly Bengaluru's. It does not claim the city is buying a measurable quantity of water.

One argument does not hold up: "pay Kodagu so Bengaluru gets more water". If a scheme is sold that way, people will judge it on litres. It will fail that test, and it will lose public support.

"The community" is really four groups, and a per-hectare payment reaches only one of them. Landholders, mostly smallholders, can be paid to keep native shade and stream banks. Paddy owners can be paid to keep their fields as wetlands, as Kerala already does. Village committees can be funded to protect sacred groves and community forests. Landless adivasi and plantation workers, who own nothing to be paid for, can be employed as paid stewards.

A law change would do more than money. Silver oak spreads because planters may fell it freely while the state owns the native trees on most farms. Giving landholders rights over native trees they plant or protect, while still protecting large old trees, would fix much of the shade problem for almost nothing.

What money can't do. Stopping a sale to a resort would take ₹5–10 lakh per hectare a year. That is unaffordable, so regulation has to do that job. The state's own rejection of the eco-sensitive area makes that harder.

About ₹103 crore a year for the whole scheme: upstream payments, co-funding of sewage plants in towns above the intake, Arkavathy farmers, and monitoring. That is about ₹2.1 per kL billed in Bengaluru, or 4.7% of BWSSB's revenue. Change any line below. Full compensation for every coffee farm would cost ₹650–3,000 crore a year; the scheme pays only where it changes what happens on the ground.

How it compares. Costa Rica and Mexico pay $28–100 per ha a year. New York spends about $100 million a year on its watershed, about ₹7 per kL. Quito puts 2% of water bills into its water fund; Lima puts about 0.5%. This scheme sits in the middle.

Not Bengaluru alone, since it is not the main beneficiary of a healthy catchment. On our proposed split Bengaluru pays three things in full: the Arkavathy payments, its own co-funding of sewage plants upstream, and its share of monitoring. It pays a third of the upstream landscape payments. Karnataka pays the rest through its irrigation budget and the forest-linked share of central taxes it already receives, and Kerala co-funds the Wayanad paddy line. Bengaluru's part comes to about ₹53 crore a year: ₹1.1 per kL, or about ₹17 a month for a family using 16 kL.

A family using 16 kL a month pays ₹17. Companies and bulk users pay the same per kL on more water. Companies can also give through the same trust as part of their water-stewardship commitments.

Through an independent trust, with payments tied to things you can see from space. Section 16(1)(d) of the BWSSB Act lets the board's charges fund anything that improves the city's water supply. But section 16(2) bars it from building up a reserve, so the money must pass each year to a trust. The trust pays farmers, committees and workers when satellite images show forest, paddy and groves still standing. It must not pay for trees counted or saplings handed out.

  • Spend the money; don't just collect it. Lima's utility has spent only about 7% of the money it set aside for its catchment. Peru had to pass a decree in January 2026 forcing utilities to use it or hand it over. Quito's fund works better because an independent trust holds and spends it.
  • Contract between governments. In China, the downstream province pays the upstream one ¥100–200 million a year, depending on the water quality measured at their border. The target has been met 12 years running. Karnataka could make the same deal with Kodagu and Hassan, using sediment and water quality at the Harangi and KRS inflows.
  • Who receives. Pay households and village committees directly. In Vietnam's national scheme, households got only about 6% of payouts and forest boards about half. Use the Biodiversity Management Committees that already exist in every local body, and the gram sabhas, though Karnataka has rejected most forest-rights claims, which limits that route for now.
  • Avoid tree-count credits. India's Green Credit scheme still rewards counted trees on Forest Department land and has no water method. Paying for trees planted would reward the very planting that can cut dry-season flow.
  • Budget for checking. Monitoring and administration typically cost 7–11% of a scheme. A pair of stream gauges costs about $4,700. Satellite checks of forest and paddy cover are the cheapest trigger for payment.
  • Kerala needs its own deal. BWSSB can build works anywhere in Karnataka, but not in Wayanad. The Kabini side needs an agreement between the two states, perhaps built on Kerala's existing paddy payments.

Its sewage, its leaks and its own old watershed, the Arkavathy. Closing the city's sewage gap costs about ₹1,900 crore, or about ₹1.50 per kL, and would stop the harm it is doing to Byramangala and Tamil Nadu. ₹100 crore spent fixing leaking pipes saves about 27 million litres a day. The same money buys 7 MLD from Stage VI and about 2 from Mekedatu. Kodagu would yield no amount you could verify. Paying farmers around TG Halli to give up eucalyptus and borewells is the one watershed payment likely to bring water back.

The home options are harder than they look. TG Halli already holds 2.2 TMC that the city doesn't use, because people don't trust the water. Fixing leaks also cuts the water that leaks recharge into the aquifer. And keeping more treated water in the city would cut what Kolar's farmers now receive. But none of these is a reason to look upstream first.

The order we'd suggest: close the sewage gap; fund the home watershed, including the Arkavathy farmers; then make a modest upstream payment, framed as fairness and insurance.

Measured honestly, the scheme would buy land kept in a working state. Over ten years that could mean:

  • native shade kept on 20,000 ha of coffee;
  • 25,500 ha of paddy and wetland still farmed;
  • the 1,214 sacred groves protected;
  • about 1,500 km of stream bank restored;
  • steady work for about 2,000 landless people.

It would also mean somewhat less silt in the dams and cleaner water in the towns upstream. It would not mean a number of extra litres at the city's tap.

The biggest unknown is how much water each sub-basin gives in each month, and how land use has changed it. Nobody has published a land-use study of Kodagu's catchment, or a reservoir silt survey since 2009. The drought-year split of benefits, the Arkavathy payment rate and the cost of upstream sewage plants are our own estimates. Each is marked as such.

Data that would settle the biggest questions:

  • Daily inflows to Harangi, KRS and Kabini over 2015–2026.
  • The Central Water Commission's reservoir silt surveys.
  • BWSSB's monthly draw from each reservoir.
  • Coffee Board cultivation costs for 2024-26.
  • Outcomes of forest-rights claims in Kodagu.
  • A legal opinion on sections 16(1)(d) and 127A of the BWSSB Act.
