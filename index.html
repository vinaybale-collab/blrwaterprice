What should a kilolitre of water cost in Bengaluru?

A family in Bengaluru that uses 20,000 litres of water a month pays BWSSB about 0 for every 1,000 litres. That 1,000 litres is a kilolitre.

Supplying it costs far more. BWSSB pumps the water about 100 km from the Cauvery and lifts it 350 metres. It treats it and pipes it to your tap. Then it should collect the sewage, clean it and send it back to the Cauvery, so the river gets its water back. Add what it costs to build those pipes, pumps and plants and to replace them as they wear out, and a kilolitre costs about 0.

Even that leaves out costs that fall on others. In the dry months the city takes almost as much water as Karnataka must send on to Tamil Nadu, and the fish, turbines and farms downstream go short. And the pumps run on coal power that heats the planet. Count those too and a kilolitre costs about 0.

The tanker market does not show the true price. It sells untreated groundwater from an over-drawn aquifer with a cartel margin. It does show that Bengaluru already pays the full cost of water. It just makes the households the pipes don't reach pay it, for worse water. Roughly 40% of the city's water still comes from borewells and tankers; question 9 notes what that would add.

About 0. BWSSB pumps about 1,900 million litres a day. Leaks, free public taps and unbilled connections take the rest. So BWSSB spreads its costs over less water than it pumps, and that adds about 0 to the 0. Losses raise every number below.

How much is drawn. The design capacity of Stages I–V is 2,225 MLD. Reported drawal runs from 1,650 MLD (WELL Labs, September 2026) to 1,940 MLD (New Indian Express, August 2026). Stage V drew only about 350 of its 775 MLD for 18 months after it opened, because the 110 villages it was built for were still being connected: 89,000 connections by April 2025 against a target of 4 lakh. The model uses 1,900 MLD.

How much is lost. BWSSB says non-revenue water fell from about 51% to 26.5%. The World Bank says 27–28%. No independent water-balance audit exists, and the figure was 49% in 2016. The model uses 30%.

Which losses count. In 2016 about half the losses were physical leaks and half commercial: water used but not billed, including 7,477 free public taps. Commercial losses are water people drink, so they belong in "what it costs to deliver a kL" but not in "what it costs per kL billed". Leaks also recharge the aquifer.

Why Bengaluru is expensive. Most Indian cities take water by gravity from a nearby reservoir. Bengaluru sits about 350 m above the Cauvery at TK Halli and 100–120 km away. JICA's design consultant gives 120 km and 360 m; the World Bank and S. Vishwanath have used 300 m. Every litre passes through pumping stations at TK Halli, Harohalli and Tataguni. BWSSB's Cauvery power bill was ₹86 cr a month in October 2025, and all its electricity ₹124 cr of ₹239 cr in August 2025.

That is ₹32 per kL billed for power alone, more than a household pays for water and sewerage together. At KERC's HT-1 tariff the bill implies about 2.8 kWh per kL produced across all loads. Vishwanath's figure for the bulk lift alone is 1.88 kWh per kL.

What is assumed. The ₹239 cr is treated as pure operating cost, which is how The Hindu reported it ("not accounting for debt repayment and depreciation"). BWSSB publishes no recent audited accounts. How the ₹28 is worked out. Each part of the system is priced at what it would cost to build today. For each part, the model works out a fixed yearly payment that repays the money with interest over the part's life, so the city can rebuild it when it wears out. Think of it as a home loan that never ends. That one payment covers interest, repayment and replacement, so the model does not add depreciation or maintenance spending on top. That would count the same money twice.

What the system would cost to build. The intake, treatment plants, 100-plus km of big pipes and pumping stations are priced at ₹7 crore for each million litres a day they can carry. Each kL you use comes back as about 0.8 kL of sewage. Collecting it all and cleaning it to the National Green Tribunal's standard costs 0 + 0 per kL of water. Cleaning it further, to the quality a river or lake can take, adds 6. Piping the eastern valleys' cleaned water back to the Cauvery adds 4; most of that trip is downhill, because the city sits 350 m above the river. Today about 667 million litres a day reach lakes and drains uncleaned, and most cleaned water flows east, away from the Cauvery. The third part. Questions 3, 4 and 5 add up to the 113.

How much sewage. BWSSB treats about 1,213 MLD in 34 plants. Apartment and campus plants, which the city requires of large buildings, treat roughly another 150–370 MLD; the model uses 250. Estimates of how much the city generates run from 1,480 MLD (Economic Survey 2025-26) to 2,130 MLD (BWSSB, July 2025) to 2,580 MLD (the minister, September 2026). The official figure is low because it leaves out sewage made from borewell water. The model uses 2,130 MLD.

What it costs. BWSSB's nine new plants, built to NGT nutrient norms, cost ₹2,071 cr for 535 MLD, or ₹3.9 cr per MLD. The sewer network is 11,894 km (BWSSB, 2024). Today's sewerage running cost is about ₹12 per kL treated, plus ₹3 for nutrient removal and polishing.

Who should pay. The model spreads sewer and plant costs over all sewage, including sewage made from borewell water, and charges each kL of piped water only for the sewage it creates. About ₹1,040 cr a year of sewerage cost belongs to borewell users. Today they pay a flat ₹130 a month. Question 10 proposes a metered charge.

Sending it back. Bengaluru drains through three valleys. The Vrishabhavathi valley flows west into the Arkavathy and back to the Cauvery. The Hebbal and Koramangala–Challaghatta valleys flow east: today their cleaned water goes to the Kolar and Chikkaballapur tanks through the K-C Valley and H-N Valley schemes, and the rest to the South Pennar. To give the Cauvery its water back, the eastern water has to be pumped over the ridge, about 70–120 m, and then flows down to the river. The K-C and H-N Valley pipelines cost ₹3.05 and ₹4.5 crore for each million litres a day they carry, so the model uses ₹3.75. Cleaning to river quality costs more than the tribunal's standard: BWSSB's tertiary plant in Vrishabhavathi Valley runs at ₹10–12 per kL, so the model adds ₹7 on top of the nutrient removal already counted.

Sending the eastern water west would take it away from Kolar's tanks, which now depend on it. Question 7 puts a number on that: on current evidence, Kolar's farmers lose more than the Cauvery gains. The switch shows the price both ways.

Apartments that run their own plants already pay for treatment twice: once in their own STP and once through the sanitary charge on their BWSSB bill. A sewerage charge tied to what is actually sent to BWSSB's sewers would fix that.

A regulator would start from what BWSSB's existing assets are worth on its books, after wear and grants. We guess that at ₹12,000 crore, because BWSSB doesn't publish it. Allowing a 10% return on that gives about ₹97 per kL. S. Vishwanath's own estimate for water plus sewerage is about ₹95. The model's ₹104 is the higher figure because it pays to rebuild every asset at today's prices.

About 0. It depends on who lends and at what rate. If the money were free, a kL would cost 0. At the return India lets power companies earn, it would cost 0. Government grants lower the water bill, but taxpayers pay the difference. This is part of question 4, not an extra cost.

The system view. India's other regulated networks are priced on one formula: revenue = operating cost + depreciation + asset base × allowed return. Electricity does this under KERC, airports under AERA, gas pipelines under PNGRB. Water in Karnataka has no such regime. The model is in real rupees, so its cost of capital is real.

The state borrows at about 2% real: a 7.4% bond yield less 5.2–5.5% inflation. Municipal bonds price at about 8.5–9.5% nominal, or 3–4% real. A KERC-style regulated utility earns about 6% real. Ofwat allows England's water companies 4.03%. The base case of 4% sits between what the state pays and what a regulator would allow.

Grants move cost; they do not remove it. AMRUT 2.0 pays 25% of new project cost for cities over 10 lakh. Grant-funded assets earn no return and no depreciation in the tariff, the way CERC treats consumer contributions. Every 25% of capital paid by grants takes about ₹10/kL off the bill and puts it on taxpayers.

A revenue bond is not possible yet. BWSSB bills about ₹2,180 cr a year net of penalties, against about ₹2,870 cr of operating cost, so there is no cash to service debt. A ₹5,000 cr bond at 9% over 10 years needs about ₹780 cr a year, and lenders want 1.3 times that covered. ₹2,870 cr + ₹1,014 cr over 485 million kL billed is about ₹80/kL. That is the average tariff BWSSB would need before it could borrow on its own name.

Stage VI will cost ₹6,939 crore and bring 500 million litres a day. Paid for with a cheap loan from Japan (JICA), its water costs 0 per kL at the city's reservoirs. With bank loans and private investors it costs 0. With no subsidy at all it costs 0. The Japanese loan looks cheapest because Japanese taxpayers, and Karnataka, which carries the currency risk, pay part of the bill. This tells you what the next kL of water will cost the city.

How a lender would price it. A bank looks at one project, not the whole system. Stage VI costs ₹6,939 crore for 500 million litres a day (Budget 2026-27) and takes about three years to build. The model follows the money year by year. It counts the interest that builds up during construction, a tariff that rises with inflation, insurance, and the taxes a private investor pays. It also assumes the pipeline starts at 45% use and takes four years to fill, because Stage V ran half-empty for 18 months.   • The lender changes the price more than the pipes do. The same pipeline gives water at ₹28 per kL with JICA money and ₹53 with commercial money.
  • The JICA price hides a subsidy. With no subsidy, Stage VI water costs ₹39. JICA's lower price works because Japanese taxpayers lend below cost, and Karnataka repays in yen and takes the loss if the rupee falls.
  • A short loan is a problem. Pipes last 40 years, but a bond has to be repaid in 15. To repay it in time, BWSSB would have to charge about ₹59 per kL in those years, instead of ₹36 spread over the pipes' life. Either water costs more for 15 years, the bond gets refinanced, or the state pays. A 30-year loan brings it down to ₹39.
  • Private builders carry the risk of cost overruns. Yettinahole's cost rose 179%. With a JICA loan or a bond, the state pays for overruns. Under HAM or a PPP the builder does, and charges for that risk in its bid. Try the overrun slider below.
  • For the sewage gap, HAM fits. Building plants for the ~670 MLD not treated today, at ₹3.9 cr per MLD, is about ₹2,600 cr; much of the real gap is in sewers that do not reach the plants. On the Namami Gange model the state pays 40% up front, the annuity on the rest is about ₹4 per kL billed for 15 years, and running the plants adds about ₹6–8 more.

Bengaluru takes its water at the Shiva Anicut, a few kilometres above the Shivanasamudram falls. In the dry months it takes about 22 cubic metres a second, almost as much as the 27 that Karnataka must send past Biligundlu to Tamil Nadu in those months. That water would have kept fish and wildlife alive in the gorge, turned turbines, grown rice in the delta and recharged wells. Added up, and including what Kolar's farmers lose if the city pipes their water back, it comes to 19 per kL. The first cost the 113 leaves out.

Some of these values are lost the moment the city takes the water, even if it later sends the water back: the gorge below the intake runs low and the Shivanasamudram turbines lose it. Others are lost only for water that never comes back to the Cauvery: the farms, the Mettur power stations and the delta. With the return pipelines, 32% of the draw never comes back. Without them, 73%.

Why the farm number is small. In about half the years since 1985 the Cauvery delivered all that Tamil Nadu was owed and the surplus ran to the sea. In those years an extra kilolitre grows nothing. In drought years like 2023-24, when the delta lost about 40% of its paddy, a kilolitre was worth ₹7–11. The average across years is about ₹2. Move the slider to see a drought year.

Sending the water back has a price. Piping the eastern valleys' cleaned water to the Cauvery costs about 4 per kL, and Kolar's farmers lose about 7. The Cauvery gains back about ₹3. On these numbers the water does more good in Kolar's tanks than it would back in the Cauvery. The better course is to keep sending it east and pay the Cauvery for the dry-month flow the city takes. Two things could change that. The Kolar figure comes from one study that has not yet been peer reviewed. And returning the water would add about 10 TMC a year past Biligundlu, roughly the size of this year's dispute with Tamil Nadu, which a court may value far above ₹3 a kL. Switch the return off in question 5 to see both prices.

The dry months carry the cost. The river flow, the Shivanasamudram power and most of the farm losses fall between January and May. Priced in those five months alone, the water is worth about 13 per kL drawn, and almost nothing in the monsoon. That is the case for a dry-season surcharge on the top tariff blocks.

Everything this page pays for nature. Cleaning the sewage and sending it back (question 5), the river, farms and forests (this question, without Kolar), and the climate and the harm left after treatment (question 8) add up to 120 of the 208. Most of the true price of water is the cost of not harming nature.

Pumping and cleaning a kL uses about 0 units of electricity, most of it from coal. The US government puts the damage from that carbon at about 0 per kL. Pollution left over after treatment adds 0. That brings the total to 0. BWSSB says solar power would cost it less than grid power and would remove most of the carbon. So the city should switch to solar rather than bill you for the carbon. The second cost the 103 leaves out.

Why carbon, and not every service nature provides? Lakes and wetlands do many useful things. They clean water, hold back floods, cool the city and shelter birds. Researchers put a value on each, per hectare of lake. You can't add those values to the price of a kilolitre, because one kilolitre of water doesn't destroy a hectare of lake. Sewage and builders do.

So the page counts a cost only when two things are true. One more kilolitre of water has to change it, and no other question can already count it. Cleaning sewage (question 5) already pays for the cleaning a lake can no longer do. Building on lakes is a land problem, and a water bill can't fix it. That leaves the coal power here, and in question 7 the river's dry-month flow, the farms, the turbines and the forests. The table below goes through each service.

The carbon price. Carbon is a global externality, so it is valued at global damages: the US EPA's 2023 estimate for emissions in 2025–26, about $200–210 a tonne in 2020 dollars, or about $255 in 2026 dollars. India's own share of global damages is much smaller, about $40 on the same basis. EPA's value at a 1.5% discount rate is about $425. The national grid factor (0.675 t per MWh, CEA 2025-26) is used because Karnataka's grid is interconnected and the marginal plant is coal. The US stopped federal use of these estimates in 2025; the science behind them has not changed.

The ladder is a steady state. Today is not. About 667 MLD of sewage goes untreated, after counting apartment and campus plants. At the NGT's rate of ₹2 cr per MLD a year, that is about ₹38 per kL billed of damage the price does not show. Bellandur and Varthur still receive sewage after ₹576 cr of work. The South Pennar below the city is a CPCB Priority I stretch with BOD up to 146 mg/L. More than 8,000 acres of Tamil Nadu farmland irrigated from Kelavarapalli take the foam.

The 103 is fairly firm. Let every uncertain number move within its range, and it lands between 0 and 0 eight times in ten. The 186 is less firm: it lands between 0 and 0, mostly because experts disagree on what a tonne of carbon costs. If BWSSB published its accounts, the ranges would narrow. These are the error bars.

Each bar moves one input from the low to the high end of its tolerance range, holding the rest at base. The P10–P90 ranges come from 1,500 runs that draw every ranged input from a triangular distribution (low, chosen, high). The inputs are drawn independently, though production, losses and sewage move together in reality, so the ranges are indicative.

What would narrow the ranges most is data, not modelling:

  • BWSSB's audited accounts: debt, depreciation, and the split of operating cost.
  • Billed volume by tariff slab.
  • An independent water-balance audit of losses.
  • Electricity use in kWh.
  • A sewage-generation figure that both BWSSB and the state accept.

What this page does not price yet. Roughly 40% of the city's water comes from borewells and tankers. That water has its own cost: pumping energy, an aquifer drawn at 120–380% of recharge, and tanker margins. It deserves its own ladder. So does treated water: BWSSB sends ~440 MLD to Kolar through K-C Valley and ~210 MLD through H-N Valley. As far as we can find, it charges the irrigation department nothing for it, against ₹10–25/kL charged to city buyers.

  • The revenue test. Whether these blocks close that gap depends on how much water each slab bills. BWSSB does not publish that, so the test cannot be run yet. It is the first data request.

  • Lifelines per dwelling, not per meter. Apartments share one meter across hundreds of flats. The unconnected poor buy from tankers and get no lifeline at all.
  • Sewerage for borewell users. A metered charge of about ₹27 per kL of sewage would cover the ₹1,040 cr a year their sewage costs. Karnataka's July 2025 groundwater rules already require metering.

  • Indexation. Escalate with CPI, not a fixed 3%.

The ₹378 figure. The note calls it the 30-year average price. Under the note's own method, the price falls over time, because the return is charged on assets that lose value each year. So the 30-year average should come out below the first year's ₹178, not at twice it. It could only rise if the city sold much less water or built a lot more, and the note shows neither. What this means for Urvara

  • Reuse is the cheapest new water. Treated water sells for ₹10–25/kL. The next Cauvery kilolitre costs about ₹55 per kL billed for bulk supply alone. Every kilolitre reused inside the city is cheaper than the next one pumped 350 m uphill.
  • The catchment is infrastructure. A Cauvery or Arkavathy water fund, paid from a small charge on the top tariff block, would be the Indian equivalent of New York's watershed programme.
  • Companies should book the true price. A firm that books water at BWSSB's ₹99 bulk tariff understates its exposure. ₹189 is what drought, regulation and carbon pricing will eventually pass through: the number for a nature-related value-at-risk or an environmental P&L.
  • Remove externalities; don't just price them. For carbon and sewage, the fix costs less than the damage. The plan that removes them is the product, not only the number.
